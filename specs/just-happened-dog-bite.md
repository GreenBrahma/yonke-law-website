# /just-happened/dog-bite

## Purpose

This page exists for the person who has been bitten by a dog — or the parent of a child who has been bitten — and is trying to figure out what to do next while also navigating one of the most emotionally complicated injury scenarios there is. The dog often belongs to someone the family knows.

It is one of the destinations the homepage concierge can route to when the classifier detects a dog-bite event in the "What happened?" input.

## Audience

The person on this page is, in most cases:

- Recently bitten themselves — at a friend's house, a neighbor's yard, on a walk, on a job, at a public park, or in their own home
- The parent or guardian of a child who has been bitten — children are the most common dog bite victims and the most common readers of this page
- A worker who was bitten on the job — postal carrier, package driver, utility worker, in-home service provider
- Reading on a phone within days of the bite, often while caring for the injured person and weighing the relational fallout of "making it a thing"

They are not adversarial readers. They are often guilty readers — guilty about the medical bills, guilty about the dog, guilty about the friendship. The page is calibrated for that.

## What this page does

This page surfaces the single fact that changes how most readers feel about pursuing a dog bite case: the dog owner is rarely the one who pays. Homeowner's and renter's insurance carries the claim. Once that is understood, the rest of the page makes sense.

It explains, in plain language:

1. What to do right now — medically, factually, and on the record
2. How Missouri's strict liability dog bite law works, and how it differs from Kansas's one-bite framework
3. What the dog owner and their insurer are doing in parallel
4. What evidence disappears, including the wound itself
5. When to bring in an attorney — including for children's cases, which are handled differently
6. How Yonke Law approaches a dog bite case, particularly when scarring or psychological impact is significant

It does not promise an outcome. It does not pretend that every bite is a case. It does not give legal advice. The "this is not legal advice" line is woven into the content, not buried at the bottom.

## Voice and tone

Direct. Plainspoken. Calm. Authoritative. The canonical Yonke Law line lives in section 7: *"We've seen this before, and we know what to do."*

The voice on this page acknowledges the relational weight of a dog bite case without dwelling on it. Many readers feel they are betraying someone — the friend whose dog bit, the neighbor whose dog escaped, the family member whose dog was provoked. The page handles this by making the practical reality clear early: the case runs through insurance, not friendship. After that, the rest is procedure.

Person-first language throughout: "the person who was bitten," "your child," "the family." Never "the victim," "the plaintiff," or "the client" on this page. The dog is the dog, not "the animal" or "the attacker."

### 2. What to do right now

A short, ordered list. Each item is one sentence, then one supporting sentence in plain language.

1. **Get medical attention immediately — even for a bite that looks small.** Dog bites carry high infection risk, and rabies exposure must be evaluated within hours. If you have not had a tetanus booster in five years, you need one. Document everything the doctor sees and does — including the bite location, depth, and any debridement.
2. **Photograph the wound before it is bandaged, if possible — and at multiple stages of healing.** Daily for the first week, weekly after that, same angles, with something for scale. Photographs are the primary evidence of severity, scarring, and recovery. The bite that bleeds most heavily often heals to the least visible scar; the one that looked minor sometimes becomes the lasting one.
3. **Get the dog owner's information — name, address, phone, and homeowner's or renter's insurance carrier if you can.** Note the dog's breed, size, color, name, and whether it is currently up to date on rabies vaccination. The owner may not volunteer this. You are entitled to ask.
4. **Report the bite to your local animal control or health department.** This creates a public record of the bite, triggers a rabies-quarantine evaluation of the dog, and protects future victims. It is not "pressing charges." It is documentation.
5. **Do not accept an informal cash payment in exchange for not reporting or filing a claim.** The full picture of a bite injury — particularly scarring and psychological impact, especially in children — is not knowable in the first week. A small payment now with a signed release closes the case before it can be understood.

### 3. The legal framework — and why it matters which state you are in

Three short paragraphs. This is the page's central section.

In Missouri, a dog owner is liable for damages when their dog bites a person who is on public property or who is lawfully on private property — without the injured person having to prove the owner did anything wrong. *(MIKE: confirm RSMo § 273.036 — current statutory text, applicable defenses including provocation and trespass, and any 2024–2026 amendments.)* The owner does not get "one free bite." The bite does not have to be the dog's first. The Missouri legislature decided in 2009 that the cost of an unprovoked bite should fall on the owner of the dog, not on the person who was bitten.

Kansas runs on an older framework. Kansas courts generally follow what is called the "one-bite rule" — the owner is liable for negligence when they knew or should have known the dog had dangerous tendencies. That can be proven through prior incidents, complaints, warnings, breed-typical behavior, or how the dog was kept. Several Kansas cities — including Kansas City, Kansas, Wichita, and others — have strict-liability ordinances that override the common-law rule within city limits. *(MIKE: confirm current Kansas case-law treatment of the one-bite rule and the current municipal-ordinance landscape in the cities Yonke Law serves.)*

In both states, the case in practice is almost never paid by the owner personally. It is paid by the owner's homeowner's or renter's insurance policy. Most policies include liability coverage that responds to dog bites — though some policies exclude specific breeds and some exclude dog bites entirely. The carrier handles the claim, hires the defense if it comes to that, and pays the settlement or judgment. Understanding this changes how a lot of people feel about pursuing a case, particularly when the owner is a friend, a neighbor, or a family member.

### 4. What the dog owner and their insurer are doing right now

Two short paragraphs.

The dog owner is, in most cases, distressed. They may apologize sincerely. They may offer to pay the medical bills directly. They may also do things that suggest panic — denying the dog has ever bitten anyone before, claiming the dog was provoked, removing the dog from the home before animal control can investigate, or asking the injured person not to "make a thing of it." None of this is unusual, and none of it changes the legal framework. It does, however, affect what evidence is available later.

Once the carrier is notified, the homeowner's insurance opens a file. An adjuster will reach out — often within days — to discuss the claim. The framing is typically friendly. Many adjusters are skilled at small early settlements that close the case before the full extent of scarring, psychological impact, or future medical needs is known. Accepting an early offer with a signed release means the claim is over — even if a scar revision is recommended two years later, or even if a child develops a lasting fear of dogs that requires therapy.

### 5. Evidence that disappears

A short list. Each item is one phrase, then one supporting sentence.

- **The wound at its most visible stage.** Bites heal in days. The photographs taken in the first 24 to 72 hours often show injury more clearly than any later medical record.
- **Witness identity.** Other people present at the bite — neighbors, family, anyone on the property — move on quickly. Names and statements gathered the same day are more reliable than memories collected weeks later.
- **The dog's history.** Prior bites, complaints, animal control reports, neighborhood incidents, prior insurance claims. Some of this is public record; some is not. Investigation now preserves evidence that becomes harder to reach if the dog is rehomed, surrendered, or euthanized.
- **Owner statements.** What the owner said at the scene — *she's never done this before*, or the opposite — can be central in a Kansas case under the one-bite rule. Memory and contemporaneous notes matter.
- **Animal control records.** Many jurisdictions maintain rabies-quarantine and bite-report files that can be obtained through public-records request. Retention varies by city and county.
- **Photographs of the location.** Whether the bite happened in a fenced yard, an open area, a public sidewalk, or a doorway. The setting can be relevant to whether the person was lawfully present and whether the dog was properly restrained.

A preservation letter from an attorney — addressed to the owner, the homeowner's insurer, and any landlord — protects evidence and puts the carrier on formal notice in the window when it still matters.

### 6. When to bring in an attorney

Plain, direct prose.

Within the first week is usually right. Medical treatment comes first; the wound and any necessary procedures take priority. But by the time the initial treatment is complete, the practical decisions begin — about the insurance claim, about the animal control process, about how to respond if the owner has reached out. An attorney handles those decisions so the injured person, or the parent of an injured child, does not have to.

Children's cases warrant particular care. Settlements involving minors typically require court approval, and the full damages picture often is not clear until the child is older — especially when facial scarring is involved or when the child has developed a fear response. *(MIKE: confirm current MO and KS procedures for minor's settlements — court approval, conservatorship requirements, and structured settlement options.)* These cases are handled differently from the start.

### 7. How Yonke Law approaches a dog bite case

Two paragraphs.

We start by handling the parts of the case that the injured person — or the parents of an injured child — should not have to handle alone. We deal with the insurance carrier. We collect the medical records and the photographs as they develop. We file the necessary records requests with animal control. We investigate the dog's history. We bring in a plastic surgeon or scar revision specialist when scarring is significant. We bring in a pediatric mental-health expert when psychological impact warrants it.

We also tell people honestly when a case is straightforward and when it is not. Many dog bite cases settle without litigation because liability is clear under Missouri's statute or under a Kansas city's strict-liability ordinance, and the dispute is about damages. Some require a fight, particularly under the Kansas one-bite rule where the owner's prior knowledge has to be proven. Either way, the goal is to recover the full picture of what the bite did — including the parts that are not visible in the first week. *We've seen this before, and we know what to do.*
