# /just-happened/product-recall

## Purpose

This page exists for the person who has been injured by a product — whether or not that product has been formally recalled — and who is starting to wonder whether what happened was an accident or a defect. The page makes the legal framework visible, explains why product cases are structurally different from other injury cases, and surfaces the single piece of practical advice most readers will not get from the recall notice itself: do not return the product yet.

It is one of the destinations the homepage concierge can route to when the classifier detects a product-related event in the "What happened?" input — including vehicle defect events, medical-device injuries, pharmaceutical injuries, and consumer-product injuries.

## Audience

The person on this page is, in most cases:

- Recently injured by a product they own or were using — vehicle component, medical device, household appliance, consumer electronics, exercise equipment, children's product, or other
- A patient or family member who has learned that a prescribed medication or implanted device is now under recall, regulatory review, or class-action attention
- A parent whose child was injured by a children's product
- A surviving family member of someone killed by a product failure
- Reading days, weeks, or sometimes years after the injury — often after seeing a news story, a recall notice, or a law firm advertisement that made them reconsider what happened

They are not always certain a case exists. Many are reading specifically to find out.

## What this page does

This page does two things most product-injury landing pages do not. First, it explains the central tension that catches consumers off guard — the recall notice asks them to return the defective product, but the injury case requires them to keep it. Second, it tells the truth about how product cases actually work, including the multidistrict-litigation structure that most readers do not know exists.

It explains, in plain language:

1. What to do right now — with preservation as the central act
2. Why a product case runs on a different legal framework than a car crash or a fall
3. How federal recall systems work and what they do and do not mean for an injury claim
4. What the manufacturer and its claims team are doing in parallel
5. What evidence matters — including evidence the consumer holds and evidence that only comes out in discovery
6. When to bring in an attorney, with attention to statutes of repose that have no equivalent in other PI cases
7. How Yonke Law approaches a product case, including how MDL coordination works in practice

It does not promise a recall is a case. It does not promise a case is a recall. It does not give legal advice. The "this is not legal advice" line is woven into the content, not buried at the bottom.

## Voice and tone

Direct. Plainspoken. Calm. Authoritative. The canonical Yonke Law line lives in section 7: *"We've seen this before, and we know what to do."*

The voice on this page is the voice of someone explaining a system the reader did not know they were inside of. Product cases involve corporate defendants, federal regulators, and litigation structures with their own vocabulary. The page introduces that vocabulary without lecturing, and it assumes the reader is intelligent and short on time.

Person-first language throughout: "the person who was injured," "the patient," "your family." Never "the victim," "the plaintiff," or "the client" on this page.

### 2. What to do right now

A short, ordered list. Each item is one sentence, then one supporting sentence in plain language.

1. **Get medical attention and document the injury and the product together.** Tell the treating provider what product was involved. Medical records that name the specific product, model, or recalled product line — created at the time of treatment — are often the strongest contemporaneous evidence of the connection.
2. **Do not return the product to the manufacturer for the recall remedy yet.** The recall remedy — free repair, replacement, or refund — does not compensate for an injury that has already happened. Accepting the remedy generally does not waive the injury claim, but returning the product means returning the evidence. Preserve it as it is.
3. **Photograph and document everything about the product.** Multiple angles. Model and serial numbers. Batch and lot codes. Manufacturing dates. Any visible damage or wear. Any warning labels. The packaging. The instruction manual. The original receipt or order confirmation. How the product was being used at the time of injury. Whether it had been modified.
4. **Check whether the product is currently subject to a recall.** Federal databases are public — NHTSA at recalls.gov for vehicles, CPSC at cpsc.gov for most consumer products, FDA at fda.gov for drugs and medical devices, USDA at fsis.usda.gov for meat and poultry. A product does not have to be currently recalled to have a viable case. A recall, when it exists, is often the most useful starting point.
5. **Do not sign anything from the manufacturer, the seller, or any claims representative without legal review.** Early settlement offers in product cases often include broad releases that extinguish injury claims along with the recall remedy.

### 3. Why a product injury case is different

Three short paragraphs.

A product injury case runs on a different legal framework than most other personal injury cases. The injured person does not have to prove that the manufacturer was careless. They have to prove that the product was *defective* — in its design, in how it was manufactured, or in the warnings that did or did not accompany it. Under strict-liability principles in both Missouri and Kansas, a defective product that causes injury creates liability regardless of how carefully the manufacturer believed it was operating. *(MIKE: confirm MO strict-liability framework under Restatement (Second) Torts § 402A and case law; KS Kansas Product Liability Act under K.S.A. 60-3301 et seq.)*

These cases are also different in structure. Most product-injury matters involving widely distributed products become part of larger groups of cases — multidistrict litigation, mass torts, sometimes class actions — when many people have been hurt by the same defect. The federal MDL system consolidates pretrial proceedings before a single judge, but each plaintiff's case retains its own facts and its own damages. Some MDLs result in group settlements with individual valuations. Some proceed to bellwether trials whose outcomes shape settlement framework for the remaining cases. Some stay individual. The right structure for any given case depends on the specific product, the specific injury, and the timing.

The third difference is the defendant. The other side is a corporation with legal resources scaled to product-defect litigation. Discovery focuses on what the manufacturer knew about the defect, when they knew it, what they did with that knowledge, and what their internal communications and design records show. Many of the strongest product cases turn on documents the manufacturer would never have voluntarily produced and that come out only in litigation. This is records-intensive work, and it takes time.

### 4. What the manufacturer is doing right now

Two short paragraphs.

The recall itself is a managed legal process. By the time a recall is publicly announced, the manufacturer has typically been aware of the defect for weeks or months, has coordinated with the relevant federal regulator, and has prepared internal communications and consumer notices. The recall is the public part of a process that began earlier. Manufacturers do not announce recalls casually — they announce them when the legal and reputational cost of not announcing exceeds the cost of announcing.

Once injuries are reported, the manufacturer's claims team activates separately from the recall process. Many large manufacturers have specialized teams that handle product-injury matters. Early settlement offers are common — particularly before a mass tort is consolidated or before significant media attention develops. These offers typically include broad releases that extinguish the injury claim entirely. They are usually calibrated to the immediate pressure the injured person is under — medical bills, lost income, the inconvenience of replacing the product — rather than to the long-term value of the case.

### 5. Evidence that matters

A short list. Each item is one phrase, then one supporting sentence.

- **The product itself.** The single most important piece of evidence in any product case. Do not return it under the recall remedy. Do not modify it. Do not let anyone — including the manufacturer, a dealer, or a service technician — take possession of it without legal review.
- **Documentation of how the product was used.** Photographs of the setup, the surroundings, any maintenance or modifications, and the conditions at the time of injury. Witness accounts of how the product had been used in the days, weeks, and months before the injury.
- **Purchase and ownership records.** Receipts, online order confirmations, financing documents, prescription records, registration cards. These establish the chain of ownership and the date of manufacture.
- **Original packaging, instruction manual, and warning labels.** The warnings that did or did not accompany the product are often central to a failure-to-warn claim, and they are sometimes harder to obtain after the fact than expected.
- **Communications with the manufacturer or seller.** Prior customer-service calls, repair tickets, warranty claims, or recall correspondence involving the same product or product line. These can establish that the manufacturer was on notice of problems before this particular injury occurred.
- **Medical records that identify the product by name.** Records created at the time of treatment that specifically reference the model, manufacturer, or recalled product line are powerful evidence of the injury-product connection.
- **The manufacturer's internal records.** These do not exist in the consumer's possession. They are obtained through discovery in litigation. A preservation letter sent early — putting the manufacturer on formal notice that records are not to be destroyed — protects evidence that will not otherwise be available.

### 6. When to bring in an attorney

Plain, direct prose.

Before returning the product. Before signing anything from the manufacturer. Before accepting any settlement, even one that seems reasonable given immediate financial pressure. The preservation steps that protect a product case are easier to take in the first weeks than to reconstruct later.

Statutes of limitations and statutes of repose both matter in product cases, and they do not work the same way. The statute of limitations runs from the date of injury and may be extended by a discovery-rule analysis when the connection between injury and product is not immediately obvious. The statute of repose, however, imposes an absolute outer limit measured from the date the product was first sold or placed into service — typically around ten years — regardless of when the injury was discovered. *(MIKE: confirm MO § 516.097 product-liability statute of repose framework; KS K.S.A. 60-3303 ten-year useful-safe-life provision under the Kansas Product Liability Act, including the rebuttable-presumption mechanics.)* These outer limits matter, they are jurisdiction-specific, and they are often the reason a viable case becomes an unviable one if too much time passes.

### 7. How Yonke Law approaches a product injury case

Two paragraphs.

We start with the product. Within the first days of a case, we send preservation notices to the manufacturer, the distributor, and the seller — putting each on formal notice that injury claims are pending and that documents and records must be preserved. We secure the product itself, photograph it in detail, and arrange independent inspection when warranted. We evaluate whether the case fits within an existing multidistrict litigation, a mass tort, or a class action — and we tell the injured person honestly which structure best fits the specific facts.

Most significant product cases involve coordination. When the right path is to join an existing MDL, we coordinate with national firms experienced in that particular product line, often serving as local counsel or co-counsel. When the right path is to file individually outside an MDL, we build the case here. When the honest evaluation is that a case is not as strong as a particular media wave suggests, we say so. *We've seen this before, and we know what to do.*
