# /just-happened/workplace-injury

## Purpose

This page exists for the person who was injured on the job and has been told, or has assumed, that workers' compensation is the only path forward. For many workplace injuries, that is true. For many others, it is incomplete. The page makes the distinction visible.

It is one of the destinations the homepage concierge can route to when the classifier detects an on-the-job event in the "What happened?" input.

## Audience

The person on this page is, in most cases:

- Recently injured at a job site, factory floor, construction site, warehouse, or while driving for work
- Already partway through a workers' comp claim that is moving slowly, or that has stalled
- Worried about retaliation, job security, or income continuity
- Reading on a phone — often during a lunch break, between physical-therapy appointments, or after the comp adjuster called

They are not looking for a fight with their employer. They are looking for clarity on whether they have one path or two.

## What this page does

This page surfaces the single most important question in any workplace injury case: *was anyone other than my employer also responsible for what happened?* If the answer is yes, there is a personal-injury claim sitting alongside the workers' comp claim — and the second claim is what makes the difference between partial recovery and full recovery.

It explains, in plain language:

1. What to do right now — both for the comp claim and for preserving a potential third-party claim
2. The difference between workers' compensation and a personal injury case
3. Who, besides the employer, could be responsible for a workplace injury
4. What the employer and its comp insurer are doing in parallel
5. What evidence matters and how it disappears
6. When to bring in a personal injury attorney even if a comp claim is already open
7. How Yonke Law approaches a workplace case

It does not promise that there is a third-party claim. It does not give legal advice. The "this is not legal advice" line is woven into the content, not buried at the bottom.

## Voice and tone

Direct. Plainspoken. Calm. Authoritative. The canonical Yonke Law line lives in section 7: *"We've seen this before, and we know what to do."*

The emotional register here is steadier than the auto or med-mal pages. Many readers are juggling fear of retaliation with a need to do the right thing. The voice respects that. It does not tell readers not to trust their employer. It tells them how to make sure they understand the whole picture before signing anything.

Person-first language throughout: "the person who was injured," "the worker," "the family." Never "the victim," "the plaintiff," or "the client" on this page.

### 2. What to do right now

A short, ordered list. Each item is one sentence, then one supporting sentence in plain language.

1. **Report the injury to your employer in writing — and keep a copy.** Comp claims have short notice deadlines. *(MIKE: confirm MO § 287.420 — 30 days; KS K.S.A. 44-520 — 10/20-day framework.)* A written report on the same day as the injury — even a short email — protects the claim.
2. **Get medical care, and pay attention to which provider you are sent to.** Workers' comp often dictates the treating physician for the comp claim itself. The records that physician creates will matter to every claim, including any case against a third party.
3. **Photograph the scene if you can — or have a coworker do it.** The equipment. The condition. The lighting. Missing guards. Posted signs. Marks on the floor. Anything that explains how it happened. Do it before the area is cleaned, fixed, or roped off.
4. **Get the names of everyone who saw what happened — and what each of them saw.** Coworkers move on, get reassigned, leave the company. Memory fades. Witness lists collected later are rarely as good as witness lists collected the same week.
5. **Do not give a recorded statement to the comp adjuster beyond the basic facts of the injury.** Do not sign settlement documents from the comp carrier without legal review — and especially not before someone has looked at whether a third-party claim exists.

### 3. The two claims that can exist at once

Three short paragraphs. This is the page's central section.

In nearly every state, workers' compensation is the exclusive remedy against the employer for a workplace injury. That means the injured worker cannot sue the employer for negligence. In exchange, the comp system pays medical bills and a portion of lost wages without requiring the worker to prove the employer did anything wrong. *(MIKE: confirm exclusive-remedy framework — MO § 287.120, KS K.S.A. 44-501b.)*

What the comp system does not do is cover the rest. It does not cover pain and suffering. It does not cover the full value of lost earning capacity. It does not cover the spouse's loss of consortium. It does not cover punitive damages where serious misconduct caused the harm.

When someone *other than the employer* contributed to the injury, those uncovered damages can be pursued separately. That separate case is a personal injury claim, and it runs on personal injury rules — different statute of limitations, different procedural requirements, different valuation framework. Workers' compensation and a third-party personal injury case can — and often should — run alongside each other.

### 4. Who else might be responsible

A short list. Each item is one phrase, then one sentence.

- **The manufacturer of the equipment that failed.** A missing safety guard, a defective design, a recalled part, a battery that overheated. Product-liability principles apply.
- **A subcontractor or another company on a multi-employer job site.** Common in construction, oil and gas, warehousing, and industrial maintenance. The worker's employer may not be the only party with safety obligations on that site.
- **The owner of the property where the work happened.** Especially when a worker is sent to a customer site, a leased facility, or a building maintained by a property manager.
- **The driver of another vehicle when the worker was driving for work.** A delivery driver, a sales rep, a service technician — all of whom may be on the clock and in a vehicle when someone else causes a crash.
- **A staffing agency or a host employer in a temp-worker arrangement.** Liability allocation in these relationships is more complex than it looks.

When any of these are in play, there is potentially a personal injury case alongside the comp claim. The work is in identifying it early.

### 5. What the employer is doing right now

Two short paragraphs.

Within hours of a serious workplace injury, most employers and their workers' compensation carriers activate a standard response. The carrier opens a claim file. An adjuster will call. A safety manager or HR representative may want a statement. The carrier may dispatch a nurse case manager. They have a process built for this.

This is not adversarial yet. It is also not neutral. The carrier's job is to manage the claim's cost. The employer's job is to keep operating. Both have a reasonable interest in resolving the comp claim quickly — and a comp settlement signed too early can affect or extinguish a third-party claim that has not yet been investigated. The pressure is rarely overt. It is almost always present.

### 6. Evidence that matters — and how it disappears

A short list. Each item is one phrase, then one sentence.

- **OSHA inspection reports.** A serious workplace incident may trigger an OSHA inspection. The resulting records — citations, photos, interviews — can be powerful evidence and are publicly accessible after the investigation closes.
- **The employer's accident or incident report.** Required internally for most employers. Often the first written record of what happened.
- **Maintenance and inspection records on the equipment involved.** When was it last serviced? Were known issues flagged? Did the employer respond to them?
- **Training and safety-meeting records.** Was the worker trained on the equipment? Were safety meetings held? Were specific hazards discussed?
- **Subcontractor agreements and certificates of insurance.** In construction and multi-employer sites, these contracts allocate responsibility — and reveal which companies were obligated to do what.
- **Surveillance footage from the site.** Many facilities record continuously and overwrite on short cycles.
- **The equipment itself.** Before it is repaired, replaced, or removed. Preservation matters.

A formal preservation letter from an attorney — addressed to the employer, the equipment manufacturer, the general contractor, or whoever else may be implicated — protects the evidence regardless of how the comp claim unfolds.

### 7. When to bring in a personal injury attorney

Plain, direct prose.

Even if a workers' comp claim is already open and seems to be moving. A workers' comp attorney handles the comp claim. A personal injury attorney evaluates whether a separate claim exists against someone else. Those are different jobs. In some cases, they are handled by the same attorney; in many, they are not. What matters is that *someone* asks the third-party question early.

If a comp adjuster has suggested an attorney is unnecessary, that is information, not advice. It costs nothing to find out where you stand.

### 8. How Yonke Law approaches a workplace case

Two paragraphs.

We start by asking who else was involved. The default assumption in workplace cases is that there is nothing beyond the comp claim — and the default assumption is wrong often enough that the question is worth asking every time. We look for the equipment manufacturer, the subcontractor, the property owner, the driver, the staffing relationship. We secure the scene evidence and request the records that explain what happened.

When there is no third-party claim to bring, we tell people that honestly. When there is one, we coordinate with the workers' comp claim — including the subrogation negotiation between the comp carrier and any PI recovery — so the injured worker keeps as much of the result as the law allows. *We've seen this before, and we know what to do.*
