# /just-happened/medical-malpractice

## Purpose

This page exists for the person — or the family member of a person — who is sitting with a feeling that something went wrong in their medical care, and who cannot tell whether that feeling is grief, fear, or something the law recognizes as a wrong.

It is one of the destinations the homepage concierge can route to when the classifier detects a healthcare-related event in the "What happened?" input. It is also a high-value AEO landing page tied to Mike's Top 25 Medical Malpractice Trial Lawyers positioning.

## Audience

The person on this page is, in most cases:

- Recovering from a procedure or hospitalization where the outcome was worse than expected
- A spouse, parent, adult child, or sibling of someone who was seriously injured or died as a result of medical care
- Reading weeks or months after the event, not hours — having spent that time looking for answers from the providers themselves and not getting them

They are reading on a phone in a quiet room. The medical event is no longer urgent in the way a crash is urgent. What is urgent is the uncertainty. Every section earns its place by reducing it.

## What this page does

This page does what very few medical-malpractice landing pages do: it tells the truth.

It explains, in plain language:

1. The difference between a bad outcome and an avoidable one
2. What to do right now to preserve the ability to find out which one happened
3. Why a medical malpractice case is structurally different from other injury cases
4. What the hospital and its risk management team are doing in parallel
5. What evidence matters and how to get it
6. When to bring an attorney into the picture
7. How Yonke Law evaluates a case like this — including the honest fact that most cases are turned down

It does not promise that this is a case. It does not threaten that it might be. It does not give legal advice. The "this is not legal advice" line is woven into the content, not buried at the bottom.

## Voice and tone

Direct. Plainspoken. Calm. Authoritative. The canonical Yonke Law line lives in section 7: *"We've seen this before, and we know what to do."*

The emotional register on this page is slightly heavier than the auto pages. Many readers are grieving. Many are angry. The page does not match either feeling — it answers it. The voice is the calm professional who explains, not the advocate who shouts.

Person-first language throughout: "the person who was harmed," "your family member," "the patient." Never "the victim," "the plaintiff," or "the client" on this page.

### 2. What to do right now

A short, ordered list. Each item is one sentence, then one supporting sentence in plain language. The phrase "right now" here means whenever you are reading this — not within hours.

1. **Request your complete medical records — every page, every provider.** You have a right to them under federal law. Ask in writing. Include nursing notes, medication administration records, imaging files, pathology, and operative reports — not just the discharge summary. *(MIKE: confirm 45 CFR 164.524 HIPAA right-of-access citation.)*
2. **Get a second medical opinion from a provider unaffiliated with where the incident happened.** A fresh set of eyes can tell you whether what you experienced is consistent with the standard of care. This is a medical question first, a legal one second.
3. **Do not sign anything from the hospital's risk management or patient advocacy office.** They may offer kind words. They may offer ex gratia payments. They may ask you to sign a release. Their job is to protect the institution. Yours is to find out what happened.
4. **Write down the timeline while it is still recoverable.** What was said, who said it, what was done, when. Names of every doctor, nurse, technician, and resident you remember. Save it in your phone or send yourself an email.
5. **Keep everything physical.** Medications, devices, packaging, discharge paperwork, prescription bottles, post-op instructions. Do not throw anything away. Do not post any of it on social media.

### 3. Why a medical malpractice case is different

Three short paragraphs.

A medical malpractice case is not built on the question *did something go wrong*. It is built on the question *did the provider depart from the accepted standard of care, and did that departure cause the harm*. Most bad outcomes are not malpractice. Most malpractice does not result in a viable case. The threshold is high on purpose — because if every bad outcome were a lawsuit, no one could practice medicine.

The case is also structurally harder to investigate than a car accident. The records that prove or disprove what happened are controlled by the institution that may be the defendant. The expert testimony required to establish the standard of care is expensive, and the doctors qualified to give it face professional pressure for doing so. Both Missouri and Kansas place procedural and timing requirements on these cases that do not apply to ordinary injury claims — including affidavits of merit and shorter statutes of limitations. *(MIKE: confirm MO § 538.225 affidavit-of-merit requirement and current SOL framework.)*

This is why the early read on a case matters so much. A medical malpractice attorney is not someone you call to start a fight. They are someone you call to find out whether there is one.

### 4. What the hospital is doing right now

Two short paragraphs.

Most hospital systems and large medical groups have a risk management department. After a serious adverse outcome, that department becomes active — often within days. They may contact you to express concern, offer to waive a bill, or invite you to a "patient relations" meeting. Some of that contact is genuinely about empathy. Some of it is about containing institutional liability. The two can be hard to tell apart from the inside.

This is not adversarial yet. It does not have to become adversarial. But it is asymmetric — they have lawyers, claims professionals, and a process. You have your memory and a folder of paperwork. The point of an early conversation with an attorney is not to escalate. It is to understand the field you are standing on.

### 5. Evidence that matters — and how to get it

A short list. Each item is one phrase, then one sentence.

- **The complete chart, not the discharge summary.** Nursing notes, vital signs, medication administration records (MARs), anesthesia logs, and consults often contain the real timeline. Hospitals sometimes send the summary version when full records are requested. Ask again, in writing.
- **Raw imaging files, not just the radiologist's read.** DICOM files can be re-read by an independent radiologist. A finding missed once can be visible on the same image.
- **Pathology slides and tissue blocks.** Especially in cancer-related cases, preserved tissue can be re-examined years later. These are kept on long retention schedules but not forever.
- **Device and medication identifiers.** Serial numbers, lot numbers, NDC codes. If a device or drug is implicated, the manufacturer record may matter as much as the provider record.
- **Informed consent documents.** What you were told about risks, in writing, before the procedure.
- **Incident reports.** Hospitals generate these for adverse events. They often have legal protections, but knowing they exist — and that the hospital is on notice — is itself useful.

Getting all of this is easier when an attorney requests it on letterhead. Hospitals respond differently to a records request from a patient and a records request from counsel. That is not fair. It is also true.

### 6. When to bring in an attorney

Plain, direct prose.

Earlier than in a car case. The statute of limitations for medical malpractice in Missouri and Kansas is shorter than for general personal injury, and both states impose a statute of repose — an absolute outer limit beyond which a claim cannot be brought, no matter when the injury was discovered. *(MIKE: confirm MO § 516.105 — 2 years from discovery, with statute-of-repose framework; KS K.S.A. 60-513(c) — 2 years from injury, 4-year statute of repose.)* Procedural requirements like affidavits of merit also take weeks to satisfy.

The practical answer: as soon as the second medical opinion suggests something may be off, the next call is to an attorney — not to start a case, but to find out whether one can be started.

### 7. How Yonke Law evaluates a medical malpractice case

Two paragraphs.

We start by reading the records ourselves. Before we engage an expert — and before we send a demand letter or file anything — we want to see whether the timeline in the chart tells a story that warrants closer examination. When it does, we engage medical experts in the specific specialty involved. Not generalists. A cardiology case is reviewed by a cardiologist. An obstetric case is reviewed by an obstetrician. We do not bring a case unless an independent provider in the same field believes the standard of care was breached and the breach caused the harm.

We also tell people honestly when we do not think a case can be made. That conversation is harder than the easy answer, and it is the one most worth having. *We've seen this before, and we know what to do.*
