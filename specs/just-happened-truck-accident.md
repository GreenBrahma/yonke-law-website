# /just-happened/truck-accident

## Purpose

This page exists for the person who has just been in — or is just dealing with the aftermath of — a collision involving a commercial truck. They are not shopping for an attorney. They are trying to figure out what to do next. The page meets them there.

It is also one of the destinations the homepage concierge can route to when the classifier detects a truck-related event in the "What happened?" input.

## Audience

The person on this page is, in most cases:

- Recently in a crash with a tractor-trailer, box truck, delivery van, or other commercial vehicle
- A family member or close friend acting on behalf of someone seriously injured
- In a window of time measured in hours or days — not weeks

They are reading this on a phone, in a parking lot, in a hospital waiting room, or at a kitchen table at 11 p.m. The page is read in fragments. Every section earns its place.

## What this page does

This page reframes commercial truck collisions for the person who just experienced one. It explains, in plain language:

1. What to do right now — medical, scene, documentation
2. What is different about a commercial truck case versus a regular car accident
3. What the trucking company and its insurer will do within the first 24–72 hours
4. What evidence can disappear quickly, and why timing matters
5. When and why to bring an attorney into the picture
6. How Yonke Law approaches a case like this

It does not promise an outcome. It does not threaten one. It does not give legal advice. The "this is not legal advice" line is woven into the content, not buried at the bottom.

## Voice and tone

Direct. Plainspoken. Calm. Authoritative. The canonical Yonke Law line lives in section 7: *"We've seen this before, and we know what to do."*

No fear-mongering. No "you may be entitled to compensation." No countdown timers. No emergency banners. The person already feels the emergency. The page provides the calm.

Person-first language throughout: "the person who was hit," "the people in the other vehicle," "the family." Never "the victim," "the plaintiff," or "the client" on this page.

### 2. What to do right now

A short, ordered list. Each item is one sentence, then one supporting sentence in plain language.

1. **Get medical attention even if you feel fine.** Adrenaline hides injuries. Many soft-tissue and brain injuries take hours or days to surface. Document everything you tell the doctor.
2. **Photograph the scene if you can — or have someone do it for you.** The truck. The DOT number on the door. The trailer. License plates. The road. The damage to both vehicles. The driver. Any cargo on the ground.
3. **Get the names of every witness.** Phone numbers. License plate. Where they were standing. People disperse fast. Memory fades faster.
4. **Do not give a recorded statement to the trucking company's insurer.** Their adjuster may call within hours. You are not required to talk to them. You are not being rude. You are being careful.
5. **Write down what you remember while it is fresh.** What you saw, heard, felt. Time. Weather. Where you were going. Where the truck came from. Save it in your phone or send yourself an email.

### 3. Why a truck case is different

Three short paragraphs.

A commercial truck case is not a bigger version of a car accident case. It is a different category. The federal government regulates how long a commercial driver can be behind the wheel, how often they must rest, how their cargo is loaded, and how their vehicle is maintained. Every one of those rules creates a record. Every record can become evidence.

There may also be more than one party at fault. The driver. The trucking company that employs the driver. The broker who arranged the load. The shipper that loaded the cargo. The maintenance vendor that serviced the brakes. The parts manufacturer whose component failed. A case that looks simple at the scene is often layered underneath.

This is why federal law requires interstate commercial vehicles to carry a minimum of $750,000 in liability coverage — and most carriers carry far more.  *(MIKE: confirm current federal minimum under 49 CFR 387.9.)*  The system itself anticipates that these collisions cause severe harm.

### 4. What the trucking company is doing right now

Two short paragraphs.

Within hours of a serious collision, most large trucking companies dispatch what is called a "rapid response team." That is usually a combination of in-house investigators, a defense attorney, and an insurance adjuster. Their job is to get to the scene quickly, document evidence on their terms, and begin building the company's defense before the people who were hit have even left the hospital.

This is legal. It is also routine. It is not happening because anyone is acting in bad faith — it is happening because the trucking company knows what is at stake. The person who was hit deserves to know it is happening, too.

### 5. Evidence that can disappear

A short list. Each item is one phrase, then one sentence.

- **Electronic logging device (ELD) data** — the driver's hours of service. Federal rules require carriers to retain this for six months. After that, it can be overwritten or deleted.  *(MIKE: confirm 49 CFR 395.8(k) retention window.)*
- **Driver qualification file** — the records the carrier was required to keep on the driver's hiring, training, and medical certification.
- **Post-accident drug and alcohol test results** — federal law requires testing within a defined window after a qualifying accident.  *(MIKE: confirm 49 CFR 382.303 — 32 hours for drug, 8 hours for alcohol.)*
- **The truck itself** — black box / event data recorder, brake inspections, maintenance logs. The carrier controls this vehicle.
- **Dashcam and forward-facing camera footage** — many fleets record continuously and overwrite on a short cycle.

This is the section that explains why timing is real. A preservation letter — a formal legal notice telling the trucking company to retain these records — is one of the first things a truck-accident attorney sends. It is not paperwork. It is the difference between knowing what happened and never finding out.

### 6. When to bring in an attorney

Plain, direct prose.

Some people call an attorney from the side of the road. Some people wait weeks. There is no single right answer. There is a practical one: the sooner an attorney is involved, the more evidence can be preserved, and the less time you spend on phone calls with adjusters when you should be focused on getting better.

It costs nothing to find out where you stand. A conversation does not commit you to anything.
