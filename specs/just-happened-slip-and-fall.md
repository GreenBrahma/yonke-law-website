# /just-happened/slip-and-fall

## Purpose

This page exists for the person who recently fell on someone else's property — a store, a parking lot, a sidewalk, a restaurant, an apartment building, a hotel lobby — and is sitting with three things at once: the injury, the embarrassment, and the question of whether what happened is anybody's fault but their own.

It is one of the destinations the homepage concierge can route to when the classifier detects a fall-related event in the "What happened?" input.

## Audience

The person on this page is, in most cases:

- Recently fell at a business, retail location, parking lot, sidewalk, apartment complex, or public space
- An adult child of an older parent who fell in any of those places
- Reading within days of the fall — sometimes hours, sometimes a week or two
- Already half-talked-out of pursuing anything, because the property owner was nice, or because they feel they should have been more careful, or because they assume falls are nobody's fault

They are not aggressive readers. They are quiet readers. The page meets them quietly.

## What this page does

This page does what few slip-and-fall landing pages do: it tells the reader that these cases are harder than they look, and then explains exactly how to make them easier.

It explains, in plain language:

1. What to do right now — and why the first hour matters more than the fall itself
2. Why a property owner is not automatically responsible just because someone fell
3. What the property owner and its insurer are doing in parallel
4. What evidence disappears, and how fast
5. When to bring in an attorney — including when the property owner has been "nice about it"
6. How Yonke Law approaches a case like this

It does not promise that this is a case. It does not give legal advice. The "this is not legal advice" line is woven into the content, not buried at the bottom.

## Voice and tone

Direct. Plainspoken. Calm. Authoritative. The canonical Yonke Law line lives in section 7: *"We've seen this before, and we know what to do."*

The emotional register here is the lightest of the four `/just-happened/*` pages. Most readers are not in crisis. They are in self-doubt. The voice answers the self-doubt with practical guidance — not by validating the doubt, and not by talking them out of it. The page assumes the reader is intelligent, embarrassed, and short on time.

Person-first language throughout: "the person who fell," "your parent," "you." Never "the victim," "the plaintiff," or "the client" on this page.

### 2. What to do right now

A short, ordered list. Each item is one sentence, then one supporting sentence in plain language.

1. **Report the fall to a manager or property representative — and ask for a copy of the incident report.** Most stores complete an internal report. Few volunteer to give you one. If they will not provide a copy, photograph the form before you leave.
2. **Photograph the hazard before it is cleaned up.** Wet floor, ice, broken tile, torn carpet, missing handrail — whatever caused the fall. Multiple angles. Wide and close. If you cannot, ask a witness or family member to do it for you.
3. **Get the names of every witness and every employee who responded.** Phone numbers, what they saw, what they said, what they did. Other customers leave within minutes. Employees clock out. The window is narrow.
4. **Get medical attention even if you feel fine.** Adrenaline hides soft-tissue injuries and concussions, especially in falls. Document everything you tell the doctor — including where you fell and what you fell on.
5. **Do not sign anything from the property owner, the store, or their insurance carrier.** Not a release. Not a "we just need this for our records" form. Not a check that comes with paperwork. All of those can wait.

### 3. Why a slip and fall case is harder than it looks

Three short paragraphs.

A property owner is not automatically responsible just because someone fell on their property. The case turns on a single question: did the owner know, or should they have reasonably known, about the hazard — and fail to address it? That standard is the difference between a case and not a case, and it is what makes these claims harder than they look from the outside.

A spill from a customer that another customer slips in three minutes later is a different case from a spill that has been on the floor for an hour while three employees walked past it. The difference is provable — but only if the proof is preserved. Surveillance footage, sweep logs, witness accounts, and the hazard itself all tell that story, and all of them disappear quickly when no one is preserving them.

There is also a doctrine that catches many readers off guard. In most situations in Missouri and Kansas, property owners are not liable for injuries caused by naturally accumulated snow or ice — only for hazards their own actions made worse, such as a downspout that channels water across a walkway or a parking lot graded so meltwater pools and refreezes. *(MIKE: confirm current state of natural-accumulation and "ongoing storm" doctrines in MO and KS — case law evolves on this.)* This catches a lot of winter falls.

### 4. What the property owner is doing right now

Two short paragraphs.

Within hours of a serious fall, the property owner's insurance carrier — or a third-party claims administrator — opens a file. Someone will reach out, often quickly, sometimes warmly. Some of that contact is real concern. Some of it is liability containment. The two can be hard to tell apart from a kitchen table.

The more important activity is internal. Surveillance video is pulled — but only the segment they think they need. The rest overwrites on schedule. The incident report is written by their employee, not by the person who fell. The hazard is cleaned up, with no documentation of what it looked like before. None of this is necessarily improper. It is, however, asymmetric. They have a process. The person who fell has memory and whatever they happened to photograph.

### 5. Evidence that disappears — and how fast

A short list. Each item is one phrase, then one timing note in plain language.

- **The hazard itself.** A spill is cleaned in minutes. Ice melts in hours. Torn carpet is replaced in days.
- **Surveillance video.** Many businesses overwrite footage on a 7-, 14-, or 30-day cycle. The clip you need is gone if no one formally preserves it.
- **Maintenance and "sweep" logs.** Retail stores and property managers often keep records of how often someone checked an area. Whether those were being maintained — and what was actually written down — is often central to proving notice.
- **Witness identity.** Other customers leave the store. Employees clock out, get reassigned, or move on entirely.
- **The incident report.** The internal report the manager wrote is often the cleanest contemporaneous record of what happened. Getting it later is harder than getting it in the moment.
- **Scene context.** Lighting, posted signage, surrounding conditions, weather. All of it shifts within hours.

A preservation letter from an attorney — addressed to the property owner, the property manager, and any third-party security or janitorial company — protects video and records that would otherwise be lost. Sent in the first week, it can change the case.

### 6. When to bring in an attorney

Plain, direct prose.

Sooner than most people think. The proof structure for a slip and fall case is built primarily in the first month, and the most important evidence is in someone else's hands. Many people wait because the case feels small, or because the property owner was "really nice about it," or because they feel embarrassed about the fall itself. None of those reasons preserve evidence.

If a property owner has offered to cover an immediate medical bill — without asking for anything in exchange — that may be fine to accept. If they have asked for a signature, the signature should be reviewed first.

### 7. How Yonke Law approaches a slip and fall case

Two paragraphs.

We start with the evidence problem. Within the first week, we send preservation letters to the property owner, the property manager, and any third-party security, janitorial, or maintenance company we can identify. We request the surveillance footage, the incident report, the sweep logs, and any internal correspondence about the area where the fall happened. If a manager wrote a statement, we want that too. The goal is to preserve before anything can be lost.

From there, we evaluate the case the way it would be evaluated at trial: did the owner know or should they have reasonably known, and did they fail to act in time. When the proof supports it, we move forward. When it does not, we say so. *We've seen this before, and we know what to do.*
