# /just-happened/active-shooter

## Purpose

This page exists for the person who survived a shooting — or the family of someone who did not — and is, at some point in the weeks or months that follow, beginning to consider what legal options exist. It is not a page that pushes anyone to act. It is a page that explains what a civil case after a shooting actually is, who can bring one, and what it means to begin that conversation when the time is right.

It is one of the destinations the homepage concierge can route to when the classifier detects a shooting-related event in the "What happened?" input. Because this page is read in a different emotional register than the others, the concierge classifier should also route here for related searches around mass shootings, public-venue shootings, school shootings, workplace shootings, and negligent security generally.

## Audience

The person on this page is, in most cases:

- A survivor of a shooting at a public venue, school, workplace, place of worship, retail location, entertainment venue, hospital, or apartment property
- A family member of someone killed or seriously wounded
- A witness who was not physically injured but is dealing with the psychological aftermath
- A first responder
- An employee who was on duty when the event happened
- Reading not in the hours after the event, but in the weeks, months, or sometimes years after

They are reading with care. Many have already worked with FBI Victim Specialists, victim-services nonprofits, local prosecutors, hospital social workers, or therapists. The page does not duplicate that work. It explains the one thing those professionals typically do not — what a civil case is, and whether it is worth a conversation.

## What this page does

This page makes one structural reality clear before anything else: the civil case after a shooting is rarely against the shooter. The shooter is often dead, incarcerated, or judgment-proof. The civil case is against the property owner, the venue, the institution, the security contractor, or the entity whose decisions about safety contributed to what happened — and whose insurance can compensate the people who were harmed.

It explains, in plain language:

1. That there is no rush, and that physical and psychological care come before any legal step
2. What a civil case after a shooting actually is, including negligent-security and premises-liability theories
3. Who can bring a case — including categories of people who often do not realize they may be eligible
4. What the institution and its insurers are doing in parallel, alongside the criminal and federal investigations
5. What evidence matters in these cases, and how the timelines differ from other personal injury cases
6. When to bring in an attorney — and what bringing in an attorney does and does not commit to
7. How Yonke Law approaches a case like this

It does not promise an outcome. It does not push action. It does not give legal advice. The "this is not legal advice" line is woven into the content, not buried at the bottom.

## Voice and tone

Direct. Plainspoken. Calm. Authoritative. The canonical Yonke Law line lives in section 8: *"We've seen this before, and we know what to do."*

The voice on this page is slower than on any other page on the site. The pace is deliberate. The paragraphs are longer. The lists are shorter. The page assumes the reader is reading carefully, possibly with breaks, possibly stopping partway through and returning later.

The voice does not match the reader's emotional state. It steadies it. Trauma-informed writing does not perform empathy — it provides clarity. The page provides clarity.

Person-first language throughout. "The person who was injured." "Your family member." "Survivors." "Witnesses." Never "the victim" except where survivors themselves are referenced collectively in legal context; never "the plaintiff" or "the client" on this page. The shooter is not named, glorified, or analyzed.

### 3. What a civil case after a shooting actually is

Three short paragraphs.

A civil case after a shooting is almost never against the shooter. The shooter is often dead, often incarcerated, often without assets that could compensate anyone for what was lost. The criminal case, when there is one, is handled by prosecutors. It seeks accountability through punishment. It does not seek compensation for the people who were harmed.

The civil case, when there is one, is a different proceeding altogether. It is brought against the entities whose decisions about safety, security, or warning contributed to the shooting being possible at all — the property owner, the venue operator, the security contractor, the school district, the apartment complex, the employer, the festival promoter, the management company. The legal theory is most often *negligent security*, which is a category of premises liability. The question those cases turn on is whether a reasonable owner of that kind of property, in that kind of location, knowing what this owner knew or should have known, should have done more to prevent or limit the harm.

These cases do not require proof that the shooting was certain to happen. They require proof that the risk was *foreseeable* — that something like this could happen, that the responsible party knew or should have known, and that reasonable precautions were not taken. *Foreseeability* is the central concept. Section 7 of this page explains it in more depth.

### 4. Who can bring a case

A short, plain section. Inclusive by design.

- **People who were physically injured.** Survivors who were shot, who were trampled in the response, or who were injured fleeing. The severity of the injury is not what determines whether a case exists — the legal theory does.
- **Families of people who were killed.** Wrongful death cases are typically brought by a spouse, a parent, an adult child, or another close family member depending on state law. *(MIKE: confirm MO § 537.080 and KS K.S.A. 60-1902 statutory beneficiary frameworks.)*
- **Witnesses who were not physically harmed but who experienced the event closely enough to suffer recognized psychological injury.** The availability of recovery for emotional injury without physical injury varies by state and by fact pattern. It is one of the questions a careful intake conversation addresses.
- **First responders, in narrow circumstances.** The traditional firefighter's rule limits recovery for on-duty first responders, but exceptions exist. These cases are evaluated carefully and on their facts.
- **Employees who were on duty when the event occurred.** Workplace shootings interact with workers' compensation. The third-party case against the property owner or security contractor often runs in parallel.

Many people in these categories do not realize they may have a case. Many people who do realize it feel they should not pursue one because others were more seriously injured. That feeling is understandable. It is not a legal answer. Bringing a case is not a statement about the experiences of others.

### 5. What the institution and its insurers are doing right now

Two short paragraphs.

The criminal investigation is one part of what happens after a shooting. The civil-side response is another. Within days of a serious event, the institution's insurers — often multiple carriers, often including specialized professional-liability and umbrella policies — open files. Defense counsel is retained. Internal investigations begin, sometimes under privilege. Public statements are drafted with care.

This response is not improper. It is, however, well-resourced. The people most affected by the event are typically in hospitals, in grief, in trauma therapy, or in any of the other states that follow a shooting. The institution is preparing for civil exposure while the people who were harmed are still trying to understand what happened. That asymmetry is real. It is also why an attorney's first role in these cases is often simply to make sure the side of the people who were harmed is being represented as carefully as the institution is representing itself.

### 6. The investigations that are already underway

A short, factual section.

Most shootings trigger multiple investigations beyond the police response. Depending on the location and the facts, these can include federal investigation by the FBI and ATF, state agency reviews, OSHA reviews for workplace shootings, congressional or legislative inquiries for events of national significance, and the institution's own internal review. Many of these investigations produce records that are eventually available — through public-records requests, through discovery in a civil case, or through public release of agency reports.

A civil case in these matters typically waits for, or runs in parallel with, the criminal and investigative processes. There is rarely a reason to rush filing while the official record is still being assembled. There is also rarely a reason to wait until every report is final. The right timing is fact-specific and is the kind of question a careful first conversation addresses.

### 7. What these cases turn on — foreseeability

Three short paragraphs.

A negligent-security case is built on what the responsible entity knew, or should have known, about the risk that something like this could happen. Foreseeability is established through evidence: prior incidents at the property or at similar properties, prior threats received by the institution or the venue, the institution's own security assessments and the plans they did or did not implement, communications from law enforcement or government agencies warning of risk, industry standards for security at that type of venue, and what reasonable comparable institutions were doing.

The strongest cases tend to involve documented warnings that were ignored, security plans that were not implemented, prior incidents that the institution did not respond to, or security spending that was cut for reasons unrelated to safety. The weakest cases tend to involve genuinely unforeseeable events at properties that took reasonable precautions. Most cases are somewhere in between, and a careful evaluation by an attorney looking at the specific facts is what tells the two apart.

These are records-intensive cases. Internal communications, security plans, training records, prior incident reports, vendor contracts, certificate-of-insurance files, and corporate-level safety policies are often where the case is won or lost. Many of these records exist; many of them are obtainable; many of them are not voluntarily produced. The work is in the discovery.

### 8. When to bring in an attorney

Plain prose.

When the time feels right. There is no first-48-hours framing on this page because there does not need to be. Statutes of limitations in Missouri (5 years for personal injury, 3 years for most wrongful death actions) and Kansas (2 years for personal injury and wrongful death) provide significant time. *(MIKE: confirm MO § 516.120 PI SOL; MO § 537.100 wrongful death SOL; KS K.S.A. 60-513(a)(4) and K.S.A. 60-513(a)(5) frameworks.)* Tolling for minors and for circumstances involving the discovery of facts may extend these in some cases.

What an attorney conversation does, when the time is right, is map the legal landscape — who the potential defendants are, what insurance is in play, what investigations are already underway, what records can be obtained, and what realistic outcomes look like. It does not require a commitment. It does not require being ready for litigation today. It does not require the survivor or family to relive what happened in order to start the legal conversation.

If a case is going to be brought, it is generally better to bring it before key records are destroyed or witnesses leave their roles — but "before" in this context typically means before the relevant statute of limitations runs, not within a particular short window.

### 9. How Yonke Law approaches a case like this

Two paragraphs.

These cases require a slow start. We begin every conversation by listening. We do not press. We do not pitch. We do not promise outcomes. The first conversation is usually about what the survivor or family member already knows, what questions they have, and whether a legal conversation is something they want to continue at this time or at another time. When a case moves forward, it moves forward carefully. We coordinate with criminal-side counsel, with federal investigators, with victim-services professionals, with treating physicians, with mental-health providers, and with any other team already involved in the survivor's or family's life. We build the case the way it needs to be built — slowly, with full investigation, with the kind of expert resources these cases require. *We've seen this before, and we know what to do.*
