# /just-happened/motorcycle-accident

## Purpose

This page exists for the rider — or the family member of a rider — who has just been in a crash involving another vehicle. The page meets them in the specific reality of a motorcycle case, which is structurally different from a car case in three ways: the injuries tend to be more severe, the evidence disappears faster, and the rider often gets blamed first.

It is one of the destinations the homepage concierge can route to when the classifier detects a motorcycle-related event in the "What happened?" input.

## Audience

The person on this page is, in most cases:

- A rider recently in a crash with another vehicle — car, truck, SUV, or commercial van
- A spouse, parent, or adult child of a rider currently in the hospital, in surgery, or in early recovery
- Reading on a phone in a hospital hallway, a rehab waiting room, or a kitchen table — often within days of the crash
- Already anticipating that someone is going to blame the rider

They are not looking for sympathy. They are looking for someone who knows the playbook on the other side and can tell them what to do in the next 48 hours.

## What this page does

This page does what most motorcycle landing pages avoid: it names the rider-bias problem directly, in calm language, and then pivots to practical action. It also makes clear that motorcycle cases are evidence cases — and the evidence is on a fast clock.

It explains, in plain language:

1. What to do right now — and what to refuse to do right now
2. Why a motorcycle case is structurally different from a car case
3. What the other driver's insurer is doing in parallel — and what their playbook looks like
4. What evidence disappears, including the bike and the gear
5. When to bring in an attorney, which on these cases is sooner than most
6. How Yonke Law approaches a motorcycle case, including the litigation posture

It does not promise an outcome. It does not deny that fault is sometimes shared. It does not give legal advice. The "this is not legal advice" line is woven into the content, not buried at the bottom.

## Voice and tone

Direct. Plainspoken. Calm. Authoritative. The canonical Yonke Law line lives in section 7: *"We've seen this before, and we know what to do."*

The voice on this page respects the rider. It does not romanticize riding, and it does not lecture about it. It treats motorcycle bias as a fact to be addressed, not a feeling to be validated. Section 3 carries the load.

Person-first language throughout: "the rider," "the person who was hit," "your spouse," "your son." Never "the victim," "the plaintiff," or "the client" on this page.

### 2. What to do right now

A short, ordered list. Each item is one sentence, then one supporting sentence in plain language.

1. **Get medical attention even if you feel fine.** Adrenaline and shock hide internal bleeding, concussions, and fractures. Document everything you tell the doctor — including how the crash happened and what you hit.
2. **Do not let the bike be moved, repaired, or scrapped — and save the helmet, jacket, gloves, and boots exactly as they were after the crash.** The bike and the gear are evidence. Helmet damage in particular can prove head impact even when scans look normal. Tell the tow yard and the body shop that everything is being preserved.
3. **Photograph everything.** The bike, the gear, the road, the other vehicle, the damage to that vehicle, the intersection, the skid marks, the debris. Get the other driver's plate, license, and insurance — and photograph their car too.
4. **Get the names of every witness.** Bystanders to a motorcycle crash often have unusually sharp impressions, because they were already paying attention to the sound and movement of the bike before the crash. Names, phone numbers, what each of them saw.
5. **Do not give a recorded statement to the other driver's insurance company.** Their adjuster may call within hours. You are not required to talk to them. The conversation can wait until you have an attorney.

### 3. Why a motorcycle case is different

Three short paragraphs.

A motorcycle case runs on the same legal framework as any other crash case — negligence, fault, statute of limitations, comparative fault. What makes it different is everything that happens around the legal framework: bias, severity, and the speed at which the evidence disappears.

Most car-motorcycle crashes involve a driver who failed to see the motorcycle. The most common pattern is a car turning left across the path of an oncoming bike. The second is a driver changing lanes without looking. The third is a driver pulling out of a side street or driveway. All three patterns share one moment — the driver's first words on the scene are some version of *I didn't see him*. That phrase is often honest. It is also often used as a defense, as if not seeing makes not seeing acceptable. A good motorcycle case anticipates this from the first hour.

The other reality is severity. Motorcycle crashes produce catastrophic injuries at far higher rates than car crashes, because there is no metal cage between the rider and the road. A crash that looks moderate in the photos often involves head trauma, spinal injury, multiple fractures, or road rash that becomes permanent scarring. The case is often substantial — but only if it is built well from the first 48 hours.

### 4. What the other driver's insurer is doing right now

Two short paragraphs.

The insurer is moving quickly, and the playbook is predictable. An adjuster will call the injured rider — sometimes within hours, sometimes the same day — to request a recorded statement. The tone is friendly. The purpose is to lock in early statements that can be used later to argue the rider was speeding, lane splitting, not wearing gear, or otherwise responsible for what happened. Even an honest answer to a poorly framed question can be weaponized weeks later.

A small early offer often follows. The amount is not calibrated to the value of the case. It is calibrated to the pressure the rider is under — medical bills mounting, the bike totaled, work missed, family stressed. The check is intended to be cashed before the full picture of the injuries is known. Once it is accepted with a release, the case is over.

### 5. Evidence that disappears — and how fast

A short list. Each item is one phrase, then one supporting sentence.

- **The bike itself.** Insurance carriers move quickly to total the vehicle and dispose of it. Once it is scrapped, the physical evidence of how the crash unfolded — impact angle, mechanical condition, components affected — is gone. Preserve before signing anything.
- **The gear.** Helmet, jacket, gloves, boots. Helmet damage is the strongest forensic evidence of head impact. Even when an MRI looks clean, a helmet with a crush zone tells doctors what to look for. Do not throw any of it away.
- **Scene evidence.** Skid marks fade. Debris is swept. Surveillance footage from nearby businesses or intersections overwrites on a short cycle. Within a week, much of what was at the scene is gone.
- **Witness memory.** Bystanders remember in detail for hours, in fragments for days, and unreliably after weeks. Names and statements gathered the same day are the foundation.
- **The other vehicle's data.** Many modern cars record event data in the seconds before a crash — speed, brake application, throttle position, steering input. That data can be preserved through legal process, but only if the case is moving.
- **Cell phone records.** If distraction is suspected, the carrier's records show whether the driver's phone was active at the time of the crash. These are obtainable, but not held forever.

A preservation letter from an attorney — addressed to the tow yard, the body shop, the other driver's insurer, and any business with potential surveillance — protects these records in the window when they can still be saved.

### 6. When to bring in an attorney

Plain, direct prose.

The same day, if possible. The next day at the latest. The first calls — to the insurance carrier, the tow yard, the body shop — set the trajectory of the case. The first decision about whether to preserve the bike, the gear, and the scene is often what separates a strong case from a weak one. None of this requires committing to anything. It requires asking the right people to hold things in place while a closer look is possible.

If a rider is hospitalized, a spouse, parent, or adult child can make these calls. The window does not pause while someone recovers.
